  invalidation" — and the MCP server subprocess outlives every turn and every
  cron wake. So once a long-lived shell reads a guide, it reads that body

  Why it's more than staleness. The /neuron contract instructs the neuron to
  fold corrected orchestration rules back into orchestrator-launch.md. The
  neuron is the writer and the reader of that file, and it is the one shell
  guaranteed never to restart. So the self-correction loop the framework
  prescribes was defeated for the only participant in it — and defeated
  silently: a stale read and a fresh read are the same successful get_guide
  call. Same family as everything else this session: the failure is an absence
  that looks like a pass.

  Fix — key the cache on (path, mtime_ns, size), one stat per call, both call
  sites (GetGuide, ConsultSpecialist) routed through a new _read_guide(path).

  - edit-then-read sees the edit
  - non-vacuity: an unchanged file is still served from cache (a is b) — so the
  fix is an invalidation, not a deletion of caching, which the first test alone
  would not distinguish

  the stamp

● Answering your question directly, and I need to correct my own framing of it —
  I described the design less accurately than the record does.

  Escrow under Google, precisely (d35). The clinical note is encrypted with one
  random data key. That key is wrapped independently by up to three "slots", any
  one of which can unwrap it:

  - Slot 1 — Device. A non-extractable key held per device. This is primary and
  automatic: ordinary daily use prompts for nothing at all.
  - Slot 2 — Google account. This is the escrow. A random recovery key stored as
  its own object in the Drive application-data folder — a hidden per-app area
  of his own Drive that only this app can see, not a folder he'd ever browse.
  Recovering on a new or wiped phone means simply signing in to Google. Nothing
  to remember, print, or lose.
  - Slot 3 — Passphrase. Optional, offered rather than required — it exists for
  the case where the coach wants Google outside the trust boundary. If chosen,
  it's an app-generated six-word phrase serving as both passphrase and written
  recovery code.

  Where I misled you: my question implied passphrase and Google were the two
  co-equal modes. They aren't. The default two are device + Google, and the
  passphrase may never exist at all. d35's own closing line: "With slots one and
  two, the sentence about forgetting a passphrase and losing everything stops 
  being true in every dimension." So the catastrophic case isn't "forgets
  passphrase and loses Google" — it's "loses every device and loses the Google
  account."

  Your answer stands and is better supported than my question made it look.

  The cost that was already put to you and is unchanged (d34/d37): because slot
  2 puts the recovery key and the ciphertext under one credential, anyone who 
  can sign into his Google account can read the clinical notes. That's the price
  of "nothing to remember or lose", and it's why 2FA on his account is
  load-bearing.
